# Intervention Codebook

## PART 1: CODING THE 5A’s

**Instructions**:
When a behavior occurs, we add a code for that behavior (indicating it occurred). Then, if there are characteristics for that behavior, we add a code for each characteristic (indicating the characteristic related to THAT occurrence of the behavior).

### 1. Asked `[ASK]`

**Guidelines**
- If the doctor is collecting data from the patient, this remains an `[ASK Start]`. 
- The ASK will stop only when the clinician converts the conversation to something like a behavior change or a praise, then it is an `[AKS End]`. 

**Example**: 
Clinician asks about woman’s current tobacco use.
- Do you currently use tobacco?
- Do you smoke?
- What about smoking, do you do that?
- Do you currently smoke cigarettes?
- What describes your current smoking?
- I see that you are smoking.
After an ask, when the clinician says something like “So how many cigarettes are you smoking?” this will be coded as part of the ask, a continuation of the ask. 
The extent of an ASK is “Are you smoking now” and “How much are you smoking”: this is the clinician really wanting to know if they are smoking and if so, how much. Anything beyond this, for example, “when did you start smoking” becomes part of the ASSIST. It could also become Advise or Assess, but often times it will transition right into Assist

---

#### 1.a Asked – Volunteered (Self Report)**

**Guidelines**: If the woman brings up her smoking without the clinician asking, we don’t want to penalize them for this, therefore we are adding an option called `[Asked – Volunteered]`.

---

#### 1.1  Asked Open Ended (Clinician Only) `[ASK OE]`

**Definition**: Clinician asks a question that cannot be answered with a yes or no.

**Guidelines and Examples**: 
- Counts:
    - “Tell me about any smoking.”
    - “I see that you have smoked in the past. Where are you with your smoking now?”
- Does not count: Questions with finite answers
    - How much do you smoke?
    - Do you smoke?

---

#### 1.2  Asked Leading (Clinician Only) `[AKS Lead]`

**Definition**: Clinician asks the question in a way that they want the patient to say yes or no.

**Guidelines and Examples**: 
- Counts: 
    - “You don’t smoke do you?”
    - “You are probably still smoking right?”

---

#### 1.3 Provoide rationale (Clinician Only) `[ASK w/ Ra]`

**Definition**: Clinician provide rationale for why they are asking about one situation.

**Examples**: Does the provider provide rationale for why they are asking about smoking?
- I ask all of my pregnant patients about smoking
- I am asking because it is important to understand your whole health picture 

---

#### 1.4 Judgement (Clinician Only) `[ASK w/ J]`

**Definition**: Clinician is judging the patient for their situation

**Examples**: Clinician is judging the patient for their smoking
- Important to consider tone 
- Provider makes assumptions or criticizes the patient for smoking

---

#### 1.5 ASK with permission (Clinician Only) `[ASK w/ P]`

**Definition**: Clinician is asking questions with patients' permission

### 2. Advised `[ADVISED]`

#### 2.1 Before beginning to advise the patient, does the provider ask for permission to advise? `[Advised w/ P]`

**Example**:
- Would it be okay if I talked to you about the benefits of quitting?
- Do you mind if I talk a little bit about why you might want to quit smoking?

---

#### 2.2 Clinician advises woman to quit.  `[Advised to quit]`

**Example**:
- Quitting tobacco is the most important thing you can do to protect your health.
- I strongly advise you to quit smoking.
- As your doctor, I want you to know that I think the best thing you do for your baby is to quit smoking.

---

#### 2.3 A clinician can also advise the patient to reduce her smoking `[Advised to reduce]`

**Example**:
- Reducing the number of cigarettes you smoke is extremely important for the health of you and the baby

---

### 3. Assessed `[ASSESS]`

**Definition**: Clinician assesses whether the woman wants to quit. NEEDS TO BE ABOUT THE FUTURE/FUTURE GOALS. An ASSESS needs to be future-oriented. Meaning is the patient taking steps to quit or reduce their smoking or would they consider taking these steps

**Guidelines**: `[ASSESS]` is what are you going to do and it must be concrete. 

**Examples**: 
- I wanted to get a sense of where you are with wanting to quit smoking.
- Do you see yourself quitting in this pregnancy?
- What would you like to do about your smoking?

---

#### 3.1  Assessed Leading `[ASSESS Lead]`

**Reason to annotate**: If they assessed, we also want to know if they asked in a leading way that discourages disclosure. When they assess, they should allow women to say that they want to quit or are not ready to quit. If they ask in a leading way, women will have a harder time saying they do not want to quit.

**Definition**: Clinician asks the question in a way that they want the patient to say yes or no.

**Guidelines and Examples**:
- Counts:
    - “So I am sure you want to quit right?”
    - “Most of my patients at least try to quit smoking. When do you want to try to quit?”

---

#### 3.2 Assessed – Volunteered (Self Report) `[ASSESS Volunteered]`

**Reason to annotate**: Similarly to Ask – Volunteered, we don’t want to penalize the clinician when a patient brings up her smoking without the clinician asking.

**Guidelines and Examples**:
As an example, when a clinician says “How are the nicotine patches going?” and the patient states that they aren’t using them and are still smoking, we will consider this an assessed– volunteered.

---

### 4. Assisted `[ASSIST]`

**Guidelines**:
- Assist takes many forms depending on where the woman is with her desire to quit. Any of these steps will count as “Assist.” Please tag all instances.
- Any smoking cessation counseling that does not fall under the other 4 A’s (not Ask, not Assess, not Advise, and not Arrange) that still part of the smoking conversation should be coded as ASSIST because we want to give credit to the clinicians for counseling their patient on their smoking. This includes conversation at the very end of the encounter when the clinician is recapping what the plan is.
- Even if the patient starts talking about these issues “I started smoking when I was young” that still starts the Assist. The clinician does not have to start it.
- A goal (that either the patient or the clinician sets) needs to be concrete. It needs to be actual steps the patient can take in the future (from that day forward)

---

#### 4.1 Reinforce her motivation and build her confidence `[ASSIST to Reinforce]`

**Examples**:
- Praise her intention. “Great that you want to quit!”
- Ask her to tell you the reasons she wants to quit. “What are some reasons you want to quit?”
- Ask what changes she has made so far in her smoking and praise her. “What changes have you already made in your smoking? That is great that you are only smoking 10 cigarettes a day now!”

---

#### 4.2 Help her set a quit date `[ASSIST to quit]`

**Examples**:
- Guide her to pick a specific "quit date." “When is a good time for you to give quitting a try?”

---

#### 4.3 Identifying barriers/solutions `[ASSIST w/ Sol]`

**Examples**:
- Brainstorm with her to identify barriers and solutions to quitting. “What might get in the way of your quitting?”
- Offer suggestions with permission if she cannot come up with any. “I have some thoughts about what might help. Might I share them?”

---

#### 4.4 Acknowledge you heard she does not want to quit right now `[ASSIST w/ Ack]`

**Examples**:
- Acknowledge her intentions. “I hear you that quitting is not something you want to do right now.”
- Remind her she is in charge. “You are totally in charge of this decision. I am here to help you in whatever you decide to do.”

---

#### 4.5 Explore her motivation `[ASSIST for reasons]`

**Examples**:
- Explore reasons she likes smoking and help her identify reasons she wants to quit. “What do you like about smoking? What do you not like about smoking?”
- Summarize her reasons. "On one hand, you smoke because it relieves stress and most of your family smokes. On the other hand, I also hear you saying you are worried about the baby and it's getting expensive. What would you like to do about your smoking?"

---

#### 4.6 Assisted set goal `[ASSIST set goal]`

**Guidelines**: 
- If they assisted, we want to know if the assist included goal setting.
- A measurable action that the patient could take to move toward quitting or cutting down.

**Examples**:
- Thinking about it.
- Reading a brochure.
- Taking a prescription.

---

#### 4.6.1  Assisted Own Goal** `[ASSIST - Patient own goal]` or `[ASSIST - Clinician set goal]`

**Guidelines**: If they assisted and a goal was set, we want to know if the goal was the patient’s own goal.
One thing we want to code specifically is whether the clinician let the woman set the goal or whether the clinician set the goal.

**Examples**:
- `[ASSIST - Patient own goal]`: 
    - What counts as woman setting the goal: “So what do you think you can do about your smoking?”
- `[ASSIST - Clinician set goal]`: 
    - What counts as clinician setting the goal: “Why don’t you try cutting out 3 cigarettes per day?”
    - The clinician can set the goal for the woman if they ask and the woman does not come up with goal AND the clinician asks permission to help her. 
    - If the clinician do not ask women to set their own goal or do not ask permission before suggesting a goal, please code this as `[ASSIST - Clinician set goal]` instead of `[ASSIST - Patient own goal]`

---

### 5. Arranged `[ARRANGE]`

**Definition**: Clinician schedules follow-up visits or phone calls to review progress toward quitting.

**Examples**: 
- You come back in a month, but I would like to see you sooner to see how your quitting is going.  
- Why don’t we schedule a follow-up visit to see how the quitting goes for you?
- Why don’t we take a few minutes at our next visit to check-in on how the quitting is going.
- The clinician may also say something like "We're going to bug you about smoking every time you come in." Which lets the patient know they are going to be held accountable for their smoking at 	the upcoming visits. 

---

### 6. Provider brings up patient’s cessation goals in a follow-up visit `[Follow-up]`

**Examples**: 
- “It’s great to see you again. Like I promised, I wanted to see how it was going with the goal you set about your smoking, regardless of whether it went like you wanted. Last time we met, you said you would try to walk with some co-workers instead of going with those who are taking smoke breaks. How did that go?”
- Last time we talked about ___ (using the patch). How is that going?

---

### 7. E-Cigarettes `[E-Cigarettes]`

**Definitions**: Clinician tells patient that although e-cigarette aerosol or vapor is likely to be less toxic than cigarette smoking:
- E-cigarettes are unregulated
- E-cigarettes contain toxic chemicals
- There is limited evidence that e-cigarettes can help you quit long term
- There is unknown safety during pregnancy
- Long term use is not recommended

---

### 8. Cannabis `[Cannabis]`

**Definitions**: If the patient asks about cannabis smoking or is using cannabis during pregnancy, the clinician mentions that studies show an association between prenatal cannabis use with:
- Lower birthweights
- Preterm deliveries
- Elevated rates of stillbirth
- Neurological consequences for children exposed to cannabis during pregnancy

##  PART 2: CODING SPECIAL CASES `[SPECIAL CASES]`

**Definitions**: If the encounter is so out of the norm that it is not appropriate to be used as assessment of provider’s behavior.

**Guidelines**: We only code for special cases: 
- Emergency
- Questions of termination
- Not pregnant
- Unusable recording quality
